# Depression

Depression, also known as major depressive disorder, is a common and serious mental health condition that affects how a person feels, thinks, and handles daily activities. It is more than just feeling sad or going through a rough patch; depression involves persistent changes in mood, energy, and motivation that can last for weeks or longer. Contributing factors include genetic predisposition, brain chemistry imbalances, significant life events, chronic stress, and certain medical conditions. Depression can affect anyone, regardless of age, gender, or background, and is a leading cause of disability worldwide.

Typical symptoms include persistent feelings of sadness, hopelessness, or emptiness, loss of interest or pleasure in activities once enjoyed, changes in appetite or weight, sleep disturbances (insomnia or oversleeping), fatigue, difficulty concentrating, feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt, and in severe cases, thoughts of death or suicide. Symptoms usually occur most of the day, nearly every day, and represent a change from previous functioning. The severity and pattern of symptoms can vary widely, and some individuals may experience episodes that are triggered by specific events, while others develop depression without an identifiable cause.

General self-care guidance includes maintaining a regular routine, engaging in physical activity, eating a balanced diet, and prioritizing sleep. Social connection and support from friends, family, or support groups are important for recovery. Mindfulness practices, journaling, and engaging in enjoyable activities can help improve mood. Avoiding alcohol and recreational drugs, which can worsen depression, is also recommended. Small, achievable goals can provide a sense of accomplishment and motivation.

You should see a doctor or mental health professional if you experience persistent depressive symptoms, if they interfere with your ability to function, or if you have thoughts of self-harm or suicide. Depression is a treatable condition, and effective interventions, including therapy and other approaches, are available. If you or someone you know is in crisis, seek immediate help from emergency services or a crisis hotline.
