# Dengue

Dengue is a mosquito-borne viral infection caused by the dengue virus, which is transmitted to humans through the bites of infected Aedes mosquitoes, primarily Aedes aegypti. The disease is prevalent in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide, with outbreaks occurring in cycles. There are four serotypes of the virus, and infection with one serotype provides lifelong immunity to that serotype but only temporary protection against the others. Subsequent infections with different serotypes may increase the risk of severe dengue. The disease can affect anyone but tends to be more severe in children and adults with compromised immune systems.

Typical symptoms include sudden onset of high fever, severe headache, pain behind the eyes, joint and muscle pain (often called "breakbone fever"), nausea, vomiting, and a characteristic skin rash that typically appears a few days after the fever begins. Some individuals may also experience mild bleeding from the nose or gums. In severe cases, dengue can progress to dengue hemorrhagic fever or dengue shock syndrome, which are life-threatening complications marked by severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, bleeding, and fluid accumulation. The critical phase usually occurs around the time the fever subsides.

General self-care guidance focuses on managing symptoms and preventing dehydration. Drinking plenty of fluids, resting, and using appropriate fever-reducing measures can help. Avoiding aspirin and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) is recommended, as they may increase the risk of bleeding. Using mosquito repellent and wearing protective clothing can help prevent further mosquito bites and reduce transmission. Monitoring for warning signs of severe dengue is crucial.

You should seek immediate medical attention if you experience severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, bleeding from gums or nose, blood in vomit or stool, rapid breathing, extreme fatigue, or restlessness. These may be signs of severe dengue, which requires prompt medical care. Early detection and proper medical management significantly improve outcomes.
