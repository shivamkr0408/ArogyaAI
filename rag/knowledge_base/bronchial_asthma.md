# Bronchial Asthma

Bronchial asthma is a chronic respiratory condition characterized by inflammation and narrowing of the airways, leading to recurring episodes of wheezing, breathlessness, chest tightness, and coughing. The airways become hypersensitive to various triggers, which can include allergens such as dust mites, pollen, and pet dander, as well as irritants like smoke, strong odors, cold air, and respiratory infections. Genetic and environmental factors both play a role in its development. Asthma severity varies widely, from mild intermittent symptoms to persistent, life-threatening attacks.

Typical symptom patterns include episodes that often worsen at night or early in the morning, and may be triggered by exercise, exposure to allergens, or emotional stress. During an asthma attack, the muscles around the airways tighten, the lining swells, and excess mucus is produced, making breathing difficult. Between episodes, individuals may feel relatively normal, though some experience chronic symptoms. The frequency and severity of episodes can change over time and may be influenced by seasonal factors or overall health.

General self-care guidance includes identifying and avoiding personal triggers whenever possible. Maintaining a clean living environment, using air filters, and controlling indoor humidity can reduce allergen exposure. Regular physical activity is encouraged, with appropriate warm-up to prevent exercise-induced symptoms. Following an asthma action plan developed with a healthcare provider helps ensure appropriate management, and keeping rescue medications accessible is important for handling unexpected flare-ups.

You should seek immediate medical attention if symptoms worsen despite treatment, if you are unable to speak in full sentences due to breathlessness, if your rescue inhaler provides little or no relief, or if your symptoms are rapidly escalating. Regular follow-up with a healthcare provider is important for monitoring and adjusting your management plan to maintain good control and reduce the risk of complications.
